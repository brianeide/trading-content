# Junior trader interview question

## Question

https://x.com/bennpeifert/status/1882081690031673800 | https://twitter-thread.com/t/1882081690031673800

Junior derivatives trader interview question, inspired by questions in DMs:
Let's say you calculate an implied distribution of returns from option prices ("risk-neutral measure", Q), and use some empirical method to estimate a "real-world" distribution P.

Is it a good idea to try to come up with trade ideas based on the difference between these distributions? What are the dangers here? Give an example of a trade that might make sense and an example of one that's problematic.

## Answer

https://x.com/bennpeifert/status/1882519684953919585 | https://twitter-thread.com/t/1882519684953919585

Many people thinking along the right lines here.

Couple of things. First, it's quite hard to measure the "real world" distribution, so you have to be wary of things like your estimates of tail probabilities diverging from implied probabilities.

No one has any clue whether the probability of some particular variant of Armageddon is 0.01% or 0.1%, there are not enough occurrences of tail events to tell you that and anyway the world is nonstationary and changes over time. 

Now, you may have some fundamental or quantitative skill in coming up with a differentiated view on real world probabilities. Let's say you're good at predicting corporate earnings. Then you may see the P-distribution shifted to the right of the Q-distribution and buying call spreads might be a very sensible trade. This is an example of disagreeing with the market about *idiosyncratic* risk and is my example of a sensible trade based on a divergence between Q and your P. 

However, one major reason for the difference between the implied distribution and "real world" distribution of returns is risk premium, the fact that money is worth more in bad states of the world than good states of the world. This isn't a disagreement about idiosyncratic risk 

And it is certainly not a mispricing. If you had unencumbered cash in 2008, your opportunities were limitless, people getting blown out of positions everywhere, near-arbitrages where you could earn 20-30% IRR, riskier trades to make orders of magnitude more than that. 

You will usually see much higher option implied probabilities of a market selloff versus a market rally than you'd find in empirical data. So a naive view where you always bet on divergences between P and Q would say you're always supposed to sell OTM puts 

But that doesn't make it a good trade! Forget about derivatives for a second -- P might say equities return the risk free rate plus 5% on average, Q says equities return the risk free rate, so there is a divergence between P and Q. That doesn't mean equities are mispriced.

It just means you are supposed to get paid to take risk. Same with put selling. You don't have some kind of special, smart quantitative edge in put selling because the implied distribution puts excess weight on the left tail.

If you sell a lot of puts in big size, eventually the left tail will materialize and you'll lose a lot of money and be playing defense, you won't have money to go after other opportunties when risk assets are super distressed 
Maybe the average returns will make this worth it, but that's just another beta exposure that you can choose to take, you should do so fully informed about its risk/reward characteristics, its not an "alpha strategy"
