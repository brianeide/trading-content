Sorry for the delay, lots of people had the right comments here and I didn't post a summary.

The basic problem here is overlapping data. If your dependent variable is 10-year forward returns, in a 30-year period you inherently only have three observations.

Ignoring for a second all the statistics that you might or might not know, intuitively you should understand this. Taking your three 10-year observations and split them up into 36 overlapping 1-year observations, or even 7,560 daily observations, doesn't change that at all. 

We could even take those three 10-year periods and break them up into almost 3 million overlapping one-minute observations! Doesn't it sound like any empirical analysis done on 3 million observations should have a lot of statistical power? 

In statistics, the technical problem that arises here is called serial correlation, or the correlation between the subsequent observations of the explanatory variable in a time series. The relationship between sample size and staistical precision relies on *independent observations* 

When you take 10-year returns and break that up into sub-samples with overlapping observations, you induce mechanical serial correlation into your time series. Obviously the 10-year forward return starting in 2000 is almost the same as the 10-year forward return starting in 2001 

It contains the exact same returns from 2001 to 2010, the only difference is that the former contains 2000 and the latter contains 2010. So you're going to see an very high level of serial correlation in the overlapping returns, which grows with the amount that you're slicing the data 

EG the annual 10-year rolling returns might be 90% correlated, the daily 99.5% correlated, and the minute-by-minute 99.99% correlated. You do not somehow create additional statistical information about the 10-year relationship you're interested in by slicing and overlapping 

If you really want to run a regression on overlapping returns, you're welcome to, but you can't use the naive OLS standard errors, which assume i.i.d. residuals. You can't even use a general heteroskedasticity correction, you need a correction for serial correlation 

This procedure is called Newey-West, it's standard enough to be included in most statistics software like statsmodels (Python), MATLAB, Stata, SAS, etc.

As for a quick and dirty heuristic, you could increase the standard errors by the square root of the degree of overlap. EG if you're using overlapping annual 10-year forward returns, you would multiply the standard errors by the square root of 10 

Note that this problem of overlapping data and serial correlation does not bias the regression coefficient, it just increases the uncertainty about the true regression coefficient, because (without correction) it overstates the amount of statistical information in the data 

Presenting a scatterplot with highly overlapping data, when there are really only three independent observations, is a first-order chart crime, it makes the casual viewer believe there is a strong relationship when in practice there's very little information
