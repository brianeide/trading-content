# Why do puts and calls on same strike have different vols on broker GUI?

https://twitter.com/bennpeifert/status/1475111874979008514

I get this question a lot in DMs so putting it here so I can refer to it later.

"Why do put options and call options at the same strike have different implied volatilities (attaches screenshot of broker GUI)?" 

They don't. Your broker is not an options market maker or arbitrageur, they're not fitting a clean forward curve and volatility surface. They don't know what those things are. They just want you to do some trades so they can sell your order flow :)

A full set of option prices and a risk-free interest rate curve together imply a set of forwards for the underlying (from put-call parity). These forward prices in turn imply a term structure of stock borrow costs. 

If you calculate naive Black-Scholes implied volatilities for a stock that is hard to borrow, you'll find that the puts appear to trade at a higher implied volatility than calls at the same strike. That is an illusion. 

If you sell the put and buy the call at the same strike, thinking it is a clever arbitrage, you now have to short the stock in full notional size. The overnight borrow rate is probably higher than the rate implied in the options you traded because the term structure of borrow costs is usually inverted, with stocks going temporarily hard-to-borrow due to excess demand for shorting, and forward borrow costs pricing in some mean reversion eventually. So the bet you now have on is a negative carry bet on the evolution of overnight borrow costs versus the forward pricing of borrow. Nothing at all to do with implied volatility and certainly not an arb of any kind. 

What if you don't short the stock against the combo? Well, obviously not an arbitrage (now you're just long the stock!), but the reason you might do this is because now you earn the full borrow yield as priced into the options market while if you just buy the stock, you generally won't earn the full borrow yield when your broker lends it out, and many brokers don't share at all.

The important general point is this:

the underlying of every option, on anything, is the *forward* to the maturity date of the option

in equities, forwards are pretty trivially linked to spot (discounted by rates and dividends)

in other assets, like commodities or interest rates or VIX, forwards are non-trivial

So to review, the analytical process is:

1) Imply a term structure of forwards from put and call prices for each maturity, imposing put-call parity. Do not make naive assumptions about dividends/borrow.
2) Fit a volatility surface in terms of forwards, not spot prices. 
3) If you use a brokerage platform like TDA, ToS, IB, etc, remember that any implied volatility information you see there is not accurate, don't try to think of nuanced arbitrage trades based on what you see there

Some people are getting hung up on American vs European options so let's talk about that a little bit. American options can be exercised early and so put-call parity does not hold exactly. Rather, it imposes relatively tight boundaries on the relative prices of puts and calls. 

The right to exercise early has a small impact on the price of most options outside of specific cases (long term in the money puts, short term in the money calls). It is rarely the source of the large confusion that this thread is about. 

That said, analytically, with American options we have to do a bit more work to construct our forward curves. If we naively calculate forward prices from put-call parity, we will get forwards that vary by strike (which is obviously wrong). 

One approach is to compute the (unique) forward price per maturity that minimizes a loss function over deviations from theoretical put-call parity bounds for American options. Then all options with that time to maturity are priced off that forward. 

The other small wrinkle here is that certain types of American options (e.g., in the money long dated puts) will have an expected life that is nontrivially shorter than their time to maturity. They'll price off the relevant part of the surface. 

But keep in mind the process. Fit a clean forward curve. Then fit a clean volatility surface. Then price all derivatives consistently off that volatility surface.

"The implied volatility of an option"

is not the same as

"the naive IV that I get if I plug the mid-point of the current bid/ask into a Black-Scholes calculator with some rates and divs assumptions and no borrow cost" 

A clean, arbitrage-free volatility surface fits the implied density cleanly through all strikes. Someone might be out bidding at theoretical mid-market (e.g. on the surface) in one particular line; that makes its mid IV look higher by half the bid/ask width 

but "the vol of the option" is the vol it is priced with off the fair surface, not that noisy/skewed vol on the single line; you wouldn't hedge the delta on that vol, etc etc
