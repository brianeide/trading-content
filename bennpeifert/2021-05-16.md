# Being Wary of Option Selling Strategies in Terms of Achieving Directional Equity Exposure Goals

https://x.com/bennpeifert/status/1393931064180043778

If someone pitches you an option selling strategy primarily in terms of achieving your directional equity exposure goals ("buying more stock if the price drops 20%", or "exiting 50% of your position if the stock is up 20%"), this is what you do: You get out of here

You sell options if and only if you think the price you are paid to sell them justifies the ax ante risk of loss on the option contract you sold. Just like any other transaction in financial markets.

Suppose you like the stock and you'd want to buy some 10% below current the current price of $100.

Option A: wait until it falls 10% and then buy some

Option B: sell a 6-month 90% put (for example)

The stock dumps 10% next week on rumors that the CEO is not in fact going to put Dogecoin on the balance sheet.

Team A buys the stock at $90.

Team B is short some options that go up in price, and loses money. Lets say the initial price was 1.75% or 20 vol, and after the move it ends up at 6.35% or 25 vol. So you've lost 4.6% of notional, compared to Team A getting in at flat.

There's nothing complicated about this. You lost money by selling the put instead of waiting to buy the stock down there.

You might say "oh yeah but I'll just wait it out, maybe the stock won't go down more and the option will decay!" This is a fallacy. You took a loss; now you're evaluating a different trade (an ATM put for 5.75 months), after having lost 4.6%.

Maybe or maybe not that trade will end up working, but your Team A buddies can go put on that trade right now too instead of buying the stock outright, and if it works out, they'll be up a lot and you'll have just gotten back to flat.

You lost 2.7x what your best case scenario win for the option sale was (4.6 vs 1.75). That doesn't make it a bad trade; not every trade is going to be a winner.

The question is: did you get paid enough premium on the option sale to justify the risk, such that if you do this kind of thing over time you'll make money?

Seems obvious? But this has nothing to do with exposure management or rebalancing targets.

Your broker told you that options are always expensive? Take a look at the last 7–8 years experience with call overwriting and cash-secured put selling, compared to owning equities.

The last 12 months or so have been better, as the COVID crisis restored some healthier risk premium to option markets, at least for some period of time. But there was demonstrably zero to negative VRP for multiple years leading up to the COVID crisis.

My point isn't that option selling is bad or good! It's that, like absolutely everything else, it depends on the price.

And many people sell options strategies to retail by describing them as "automated" buy and sell targets for actual equity exposure, and abstracting entirely away from the price you're getting paid and whether it justifies the risk.
